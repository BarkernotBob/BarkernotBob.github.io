The Central Tension

The Protestant Reformation recovered sola Scriptura but split sharply on the fourth commandment. At one pole, Lutheranism declared the Sabbath ordinance strictly Mosaic-ceremonial and abrogated in Christ. At the other, English Puritanism articulated a fully developed jure divino Sabbatarianism in which one day in seven was a perpetual moral commandment — transferred by divine authority to Sunday at the resurrection. Between these poles, Continental Reformed theology occupied a mediating position: Sunday worship theologically warranted, but recreation permitted. The Westminster Confession (1646) crystallized the Puritan apex. Its decline over the following four centuries was driven less by better exegesis than by industrialization, the secular weekend, and the gradual adoption of theological frameworks — New Covenant Theology, Christian liberty arguments — that ratified what culture had already accomplished.

The Pivotal Question

The deepest fault line is not about day (Saturday vs. Sunday) but about basis: Is the fourth commandment a moral and perpetual law binding all ages, or a ceremonial/Mosaic law fulfilled and abrogated in Christ? The Westminster tradition answers: moral and perpetual, with the specific day as alterable positive law transferred by divine authority to the first day. The Lutheran and New Covenant traditions answer: ceremonial/Mosaic, therefore abrogated. The Continental Reformed tradition occupies middle ground — affirming the day's theological significance without the strict whole-day prohibition.
