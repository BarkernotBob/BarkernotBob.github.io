1. Credit cards
    1. Credit cards are a polarizing topic, and it's no surprise. Mismanagement of a credit card is one of the quickest and easiest ways to plunge yourself into financial turmoil. To make matters worse, it's completely legal to take on insurmountable debt and many financial institutions *encourage* people to take on credit cards and other forms of debt in the hopes that you will pay them interest on your charges. Here's a few statistics to illustrate the point:
        1. Out of 219 million American adults who used a credit card, 103 million carried debt on their credit card at some point in the year. That's 47%. To put it another way, based on the averages here, if 2 of you left work today and opened up a new credit card, most likely one of you would carry a balance and pay interest on that card within the next year.
        2. The average credit card debt in Indiana is $6,214.
        3. Credit cards charge an average interest rate of about 22%
        4. [Ramsey Article](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/debt/the-truth-about-credit-card-debt#cookie-banner)
    2. If you think I'm a credit card hater I'm not. Credit cards are the only thing I use for my purchases and I would be very frustrated if I had to switch to cash or even a debit card. But credit cards are tools not to be trifled with. Similar to a chainsaw, if you're not careful, you can just as easily lop of that pesky branch as you can your own arm. And unfortunately, while the chainsaw maker is incentivized to make their product as safe as possible and provide you clear instructions for use, the credit card issuer is incentivized to make it easy for you to climb into debt, and hard for you to climb out. And my hope is not to tell you what you should or should not do with your money, but it is to ensure that we all have enough understanding about this to make informed decisions.
    3. Mechanics of a credit card
        1. A credit card is an agreement between 2 parties. A lender agrees to provide a borrower with a set amount of credit. This credit can be used for any purchase, and is expected to be paid back in roughly a month. 
        2. Now, last week we talked about the time value of money and how, all other things being equal, we prefer present satisfaction to future satisfaction. So what benefit does the lender get from floating their money out to you? 2 things
            1. Every time you swipe your card, between 1.5% and 3.5% of your charge is sent to the credit card company. So when you insert your credit card to spend $100 at Costco, only about $98 goes to Costco and the other $2 goes to the credit card company.
            2. The other way the credit card company makes money is off of you *if* you carry a balance. Carrying a balance is when you place charges on your credit card but don't pay off the whole balance when it's due. When this happens, the credit card issuer begins charging you interest of about 22% on the balance until you pay it off. So, let's look at a quick example of how this interest impacts the total amount of money you owe:

        3. Timeline of a credit card along with some definitions
            1. You have a **Statement Cycle** which can start on any date and lasts for about 1 month. All of your purchases from this cycle are grouped together.
            2. Once the cycle ends your credit card issuer will let you know what your **Statement Balance** was and you will have a few weeks until the **Payment Due Date**
                1. If you pay the full statement balance before the due date, you will not owe any interest.
                2. If you pay less than the balance due but at least the **Minimum Payment**, you will begin accruing interest on your balance. The minimum payment is usually a very small fraction of the total balance, usually the greater of $25 or 2% of your total balance. 
                3. If you don't pay at all or pay less than the minimum payment, you will incur further fees and interest, and your credit score will drop substantially.
            3. And the cycle repeats.
    5. Best Practices
        1. Treat it like cash.
            1. Essentially, don't buy something with a credit card if you couldn't buy it with cash in the bank.
            2. Consider the psychological effects of paying with a credit card.
        2. Automate EVERYTHING. Set alerts for spending, due dates, and payments processing.
        3. Only take on interest after careful consideration
        4. Understand how your credit score is impacted.

2. Credit scores
    1. Your credit score is meant to be an indicator of how likely it is that you will faithfully pay back debts to a lender. Since no one can see the future, lenders look at how you've handled debt in the past to project how you'll handle debt in the future. 
    2. How can a credit score impact you?
        1. Buying a house with a mortgage or a car with a loan? With a higher credit score, you'll be more likely to be approved, with a lower interest rate, and the ability to take out larger loans if needed.
        2. Renting an apartment? A good credit score can increase your likelihood of approval and lower your security deposit. 
        3. Opening a credit card? A good credit score increases your likelihood of approval, for better credit cards, and at lower interest rates. 
    3. How is your credit score calculated, and what can you do to increase it?
        1. Payment History - 35% | Pay at least the minimum payment every time.
        2. Credit Utilization / Amount Owed - 30% | Credit currently used / Total Available Credit. If you pay off large purchases immediately instead of waiting until your statement is posted, you can lower your credit utilization and increase your credit score. Any credit utilization above 30% will dramatically lower your score, and ideally you want to shoot for below 10% for maximum score.
        3. Credit History - 15% | There's no shortcut here. This is just looking at your track record. For this part of your score, just know that if you have a credit card with no annual fee, it's better to keep that card open for a long time rather than close it because it will increase the length of your credit history.
        4. New Credit - 10% | This is just looking at your very recent behavior. If you open a few cards or loans in a short time span, your score will decrease but only temporarily.
        5. Credit Mix - 10% | Your score can slightly increase if you have a mix of *types* of loans, such as mortgage, CC, car loan, etc. Small impact to your credit score and generally there's no time where you would want to change your behavior to increase this part of your score.

1. Lifestyle creep
    1. In short, lifestyle creep is when your expenses rise each time your Income does (or each time you receive an irregular lump sum of cash)
    2. Wealth is only built when you spend *less* than you receive. It's that simple.
