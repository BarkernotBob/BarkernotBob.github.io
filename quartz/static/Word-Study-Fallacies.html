An interactive word study

“The Greek word here really means…”

If you've ever looked up a Strong's number, you were reaching for something genuinely good: the words behind the words. You wanted to stand as close to the original text as you could get. That instinct deserves to be honored, not scolded — and this little experiment is built for people who have it.

Here is the experiment. You're going to translate one real verse of the New Testament using nothing but Strong's entries, exactly as printed — every option in front of you will come straight from the book.

Which verse? That stays sealed until you've translated it yourself, so nothing you already know about it can help. No Greek knowledge required.

Along the way, three honest questions:

  1. What kind of book is Strong's, really — and what did Strong himself say it was?
  2. Can a list of English words carry you all the way to a verse's meaning?
  3. Do a word's parts reveal what it means? (We'll test this on a language you already speak.)

Step 2 · The comparison

How did it go?

The same verse — Luke 7:1, unsealed — from a committee of Greek scholars (ESV)
“After he had finished all his sayings in the hearing of the people, he entered Capernaum.”

Why this verse? Luke 7:1 is an ordinary hinge between two scenes, with no doctrine hanging on it. It was chosen precisely because it's ordinary: whatever you just experienced came from the method, not from a hard verse.

So what happened?

Nothing went wrong on your end — this happens to everyone who tries it. Every option you saw really was in the entries. The gap comes from three things a sentence needs that the entries simply don't carry:

  • Word endings. In Greek, the ending of a word does the work that word order does in English: it tells you who did the action and when. The verse's second word doesn't just mean “finish” — its ending says he finished, in the past: “he had finished.” Strong's prints every word in its plain dictionary form, so the endings — and everything they carry — are invisible.
  • The missing option. The verse's sixth word, αὐτοῦ, means “his” here. Open its entry and scan the thirty-odd options again — “his” isn't one of them. The one word this sentence needs most never made the list.
  • Figures of speech. Word for word, the middle of the verse says “into the ears of the people.” That's simply how Greek says “in their hearing” — the way English says “keep an eye on.” A word-at-a-time list has no way to tell you when several words are acting together as one phrase.

Step 3 · Read the manual

What Strong's says it is

James Strong built something remarkable: an index that lets a reader with no Greek training find every verse where a given word occurs in the King James Version. For 1890, that was a landmark of scholarship, and it still works beautifully. The numbered entries in the back were his lookup key. Here's the anatomy of one — tap each part:

4137. πληρόω plērŏō, play-rŏ´-o; from 4134; to make replete, i.e. (lit.) to cram (a net), level up (a hollow), or (fig.) to furnish, satisfy, execute (an office), finish (a period or task), verify (or coincide with a prediction), etc.:— accomplish, × after, (be) complete, end, expire, fill (up), fulfil, (be, make) full (come), fully preach, perfect, supply.

Tap a label above to see what that part of the entry actually is.

The punchline: by the book's own stated convention, everything after the :— mark (the list we tend to read aloud as “the Greek word means…”) is a record of how the KJV translators already rendered it. So when we use that list to look behind our English Bible, we aren't reaching the Greek. We're reaching an older English Bible. Strong never claimed otherwise; his book has simply been handed a job it never applied for.

Step 5 · Back to the Greek

Greek plays by the same rules

Greek built big words the same way English does — by gluing smaller words together. And just like in English, sometimes the glued-together word means exactly what its parts say, and sometimes it wandered off long ago and picked up a life of its own. Nothing about the parts themselves tells you which kind you're holding. Three real examples:

εἰσέρχομαι — the parts tell the truth

This word is in your verse. εἰς means into; ἔρχομαι means come. Glue them together and you get “come into” — that is, enter. And that's exactly what it means, everywhere it's used. Like flowerpot: the parts tell the whole story.

ἐπειδή — the parts fall behind

Also from your verse — it was your very first word. ἐπεί means when; δή means indeed. Add them up and you'd expect “when indeed.” But by New Testament times the word had settled into meaning since or because. The parts are really there — they just can't tell you what the word grew up to mean. Like benchmark: real parts, and you'd still never guess the meaning from them.

δύναμις — a famous example

You may have heard it preached, warmly and sincerely: “The gospel is the dynamite of God — that's the Greek word dynamis in Romans 1:16!” The image lands; the love for the text is real. The difficulty is only this: dynamite was named in 1867, from the Greek — not the other way around. Paul couldn't have been thinking of explosives, and his word suggests steady, effective power more than detonation. Word history runs forward only. Reading a word's descendants back into an author's mouth is the same slip as deriving butterflies from butter — easy to make, and worth learning to avoid.

Etymology is a clue about where a word came from — a genuinely interesting one. It just isn't proof of what a speaker meant by it. Meaning lives in usage, and usage is exactly what translators and lexicographers spend their careers observing.
Step 6 · Takeaways

What to keep

  1. Keep the hunger. Upgrade the tools. Wanting to get closer to the original words is one of the best instincts a Bible reader can have. It deserves instruments built for the job.
  2. Strong's is an index, and a very good one. Use it for what Strong built it to do: find every place a word occurs, and see how the KJV handled it. The English lists after :— are translation history, not a menu of meanings — Strong's own × and + marks say as much.
  3. Translation is not lookup. You watched a verse come apart with a full set of “meanings” in hand. Grammar, idiom, and context did all the real work in “After he had finished all his sayings…” — and none of those live in a numbered list.
  4. Word parts are a hint, not a verdict. εἰσέρχομαι is a flowerpot; δύναμις-as-dynamite is a butterfly. The only way to know which kind you're holding is to watch the word in use.

A better way in

Read several good translations side by side — ESV, NIV, NASB, NRSV. Each is a committee of people who have spent their lives reading Greek. Where they agree, the Greek is clear. Where they differ, you've found a genuinely open question — the very thing word studies hope to uncover — and you found it on solid ground. And if the hunger runs deeper still, the door is open: real lexicons, or even first-year Greek, welcome anyone willing to walk in. The original words are worth it.
