Head Coverings in Christian Tradition

From Paul's letter to Corinth through 2,000 years of Church history — how Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, and Anabaptist traditions have interpreted, debated, codified, and abandoned the practice of women's head coverings in worship.

Navigate by Section

The Central Question

At the heart of this 2,000-year debate lies a single passage: 1 Corinthians 11:2–16. Paul grounds the practice in creation order, the image of God, and angelic observation — yet also appeals to "nature" and "custom." The question that has divided exegetes ever since: are these grounds timeless and universal, or culturally conditioned to first-century Corinth?

The patristic consensus was virtually unanimous — every major Church Father from Irenaeus to Augustine treated the covering as a binding, continuing obligation. This consensus held across both East and West for over 1,500 years. The fracture came not from a theological revolution but from second-wave feminism, the misreported demise of Canon 1262, and the broader Western cultural turn of the 1960s.

Today the landscape is fragmented: Eastern Orthodox and traditional Catholics retain the practice as normative; Conservative Anabaptists treat it as an ordinance; most Western Protestants have abandoned it as culturally relative; a growing subset of younger Catholics and Lutherans has revived it as voluntary reverence.
